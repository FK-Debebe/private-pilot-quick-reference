Pilot Qualifications & Aeronautical Experience

Private Pilot Airplane Single-Engine Land — eligibility, experience, privileges, limitations, and PIC responsibilities.

Eligibility — §61.103

  • At least 17 years old.
  • Read, speak, write, and understand English.
  • Receive the required ground/flight training and instructor endorsements.
  • Pass the Private Pilot knowledge test.
  • Meet the aeronautical experience requirements of §61.109.
  • Hold the appropriate student, sport, recreational, or other pilot certificate as applicable.
  • Hold an appropriate medical qualification or comply with BasicMed when permitted.

PIC Responsibilities

  • Carry the required pilot certificate, photo identification, and medical qualification when required.
  • The PIC is responsible for and is the final authority over the operation of the aircraft.
  • The PIC is responsible for determining that the aircraft is airworthy before flight.
  • In an in-flight emergency, the PIC may deviate from regulations to the extent necessary to meet the emergency.

Private Pilot Privileges & Limitations — §61.113

Common Privileges / Exceptions

  • Act as PIC within Private Pilot privileges.
  • Business/employment flight may be permitted when the flight is incidental to the business and passengers/property are not carried for compensation or hire.
  • Specified charitable/community flights may be permitted.
  • An aircraft salesperson with at least 200 hours may demonstrate an aircraft to a prospective buyer.
  • Other specific exceptions exist; consult the regulation for the operation planned.

Key Limitation

A Private Pilot generally may not act as PIC carrying passengers or property for compensation or hire and may not pay less than the required pro-rata share of allowable operating expenses when sharing expenses with passengers.

Cross-Country Planning Sequence

  1. Determine route, terrain and airspace; avoid prohibited/restricted areas as appropriate.
  2. Choose clearly identifiable checkpoints and note suitable airports and emergency landing options.
  3. Determine true course, distance and magnetic variation for each leg.
  4. Select altitudes considering direction of flight, terrain, airspace, winds and aircraft performance.
  5. Record communications frequencies, traffic patterns, NOTAMs and airport information.
  6. Obtain current weather; compute wind-correction angle, groundspeed, ETE and fuel burn.
  7. Use POH performance data for takeoff, climb, cruise and landing planning.
  8. Review fuel availability, diversion triggers and alternatives.

Weather Theory & Hazards

Know what creates the weather, what it looks like, and what it does to the airplane.

Pressure Systems

  • High: generally clockwise circulation, sinking air, more stable/clear conditions.
  • Low: generally counterclockwise circulation, rising air, more cloud/precipitation and instability.

Standard Lapse / Pressure Rule of Thumb

  • Temperature decreases about 2°C per 1,000 ft.
  • Pressure decreases about 1 inHg per 1,000 ft in the common study approximation.

Fronts

FrontTypical Study Cue
WarmStratiform clouds, reduced visibility, widespread precipitation.
ColdMore abrupt lifting, towering cumulus/thunderstorms possible, generally better visibility behind.
StationaryLittle movement; mixed warm/cold-front characteristics.
OccludedComplex weather; potentially significant/severe conditions.

Cloud Naming

Cumulus = heaped/puffy • Stratus = layered • Cirrus = high/wispy • Nimbus = rain-bearing • Lenticular = lens-shaped mountain-wave cloud. Height prefixes include alto- for middle cloud and cirro- for high cloud.

Thunderstorms

Ingredients

Moisture • unstable lapse rate • lifting action.

Stages

Cumulus: updrafts • Mature: updrafts + downdrafts • Dissipating: downdrafts dominate.

Hazards

Turbulence • microbursts/wind shear • hail • lightning • icing • heavy precipitation.

Icing

TypeDescription
ClearSmooth/transparent, associated with larger supercooled droplets and slower freezing.
RimeRough, milky/opaque ice from rapid freezing of smaller droplets.
MixedCombination of clear and rime characteristics.
FrostIce-crystal deposit on a cold surface; must be removed from critical airfoil surfaces before takeoff.

Performance effects emphasized in the guides: increased weight and drag, reduced lift/wing efficiency, and reduced propeller/engine effectiveness.

Fog

Radiation: ground cools moist air • Advection: warm moist air moves over a colder surface • Steam: cold dry air moves over warmer water • Upslope: moist air is forced upslope and cools.

  • A VOR radial is a magnetic bearing FROM the station.
  • The aircraft can be on the same radial regardless of heading.

  • WAAS: augmentation using ground-reference corrections to improve accuracy/integrity.

  • Maintain a sterile cockpit during taxi, takeoff and landing.

  • Ask ATC for clarification or progressive taxi if unsure.

Wake Turbulence

Wingtip vortices are strongest behind aircraft that are heavy, clean and slow. For takeoff behind a larger aircraft, rotate before its rotation point and remain upwind/above its path. For landing, remain above its approach path and land beyond its touchdown point when appropriate.

Aerodynamics, Turns, Stalls & Stability

The detailed concepts behind the oral-exam questions: force balance, angle of attack, load factor, left-turning tendencies, stall and stability.

Four Forces

LiftUpward aerodynamic force.
WeightGravity acting downward.
ThrustForward propulsive force.
DragRearward aerodynamic resistance.

Airfoil & Angle of Attack

Angle of attack (AoA) is the angle between the wing chord line and the relative wind. A stall occurs when the critical AoA is exceeded, regardless of indicated airspeed, pitch attitude or power setting.

Drag

Parasite Drag

Drag caused by the airplane moving through the air; increases as speed increases.

Induced Drag

Byproduct of lift; most significant at low airspeeds and high AoA.

Turns, Load Factor & Stall Speed

Banking tilts the lift vector. In a level turn, total lift must increase so the vertical component still supports weight. Increasing bank angle therefore increases load factor and raises stall speed. The study guides use a 60° bank as the classic example of a 2-G level turn.

Slip vs. Skid

ConditionStudy Cue
SlipTurn rate is insufficient for the bank; horizontal lift exceeds the turn's outward tendency.
SkidTurn rate is excessive for the bank; especially hazardous near stall because yaw can contribute to spin entry.

Four Left-Turning Tendencies

  1. Torque: propeller rotation produces an opposite rolling tendency.
  2. Gyroscopic precession: force applied to a rotating propeller/gyro manifests approximately 90° ahead in the direction of rotation; most noticeable in certain pitch changes and tailwheel operations.
  3. Spiraling slipstream: propwash spirals around the fuselage and strikes the vertical tail, yawing the airplane left.
  4. P-factor: at high AoA the descending propeller blade develops greater thrust, yawing the nose left.

Stall Situations

  • Power-off / approach stall: increasing AoA as airspeed is reduced until critical AoA is exceeded.
  • Power-on / departure stall: critical AoA can be exceeded during high-power, high-pitch conditions.
  • Turning / accelerated stall: higher load factor increases the airspeed at which the critical AoA may be reached.

1Intake
2Compression
3Power
4Exhaust

Magnetos & Ignition

  • Magnetos generate ignition energy independently of the aircraft electrical system.
  • They operate while the crankshaft is rotating.
  • A broken/disconnected magneto grounding (“P-lead”) circuit can leave a magneto hot even with the ignition switch OFF; treat the propeller as potentially live.

Fuel-Air Mixture

Mixture is the ratio of fuel mass to air mass in the combustible mixture. The guides emphasize correct leaning with altitude/cruise operation and note that excessively rich mixtures at low power can promote spark-plug fouling.

Detonation vs. Preignition

Detonation

Abnormal explosive combustion after normal ignition. Contributing factors in the guides include improper fuel grade and high-power/lean or high-manifold-pressure/low-RPM combinations.

Preignition

Fuel-air mixture ignites before the normal spark event, commonly from a hot spot or glowing deposit. Can cause roughness, loss of power and high operating temperature.

Propellers

Fixed pitch: blade angle is fixed by design. Constant speed: governor automatically changes blade angle to maintain selected RPM; pilot typically has throttle and propeller controls.

Electrical System

Engine-driven alternator/generator powers electrical loads and charges the battery; the battery can provide temporary backup power if generation is lost. The exact bus, voltage and failure indications are aircraft-specific.

Gyroscopic Principles

Rigidity in space — a spinning gyro resists changes to its plane. Precession — a force applied to a gyro manifests approximately 90° later in the direction of rotation.

Traditional Instruments

  • Attitude indicator: pitch and bank.
  • Heading indicator / directional gyro: stabilized heading reference; periodically aligned with the magnetic compass in traditional installations.
  • Turn coordinator: rate of turn and rate of roll; commonly electrically powered.

Magnetic Compass

Aeromedical Factors & Illusions

Recognize symptoms early, use instruments when sensations conflict, and do not let physiology become an aircraft-control problem.

Hypoxia

Insufficient oxygen reaching body tissues. Symptoms in the guides include headache, dizziness, euphoria, impaired judgment and cyanosis. Corrective action: use oxygen when available/appropriate and descend to a lower altitude.

TypeMeaning
HypoxicNot enough oxygen available to the body, such as at altitude.
HypemicBlood cannot carry enough oxygen, such as with carbon-monoxide exposure.
StagnantOxygen-rich blood is not circulating adequately.
HistotoxicCells cannot effectively use oxygen, such as with alcohol/drugs.

Hyperventilation

Excessive breathing reduces carbon dioxide in the blood and can cause dizziness, tingling, light-headedness and symptoms resembling hypoxia. Slow breathing and regain a controlled breathing rate; if uncertain, the source guides emphasize treating the condition conservatively as possible hypoxia.

Carbon Monoxide

Colorless, odorless, tasteless combustion gas. Symptoms include headache, nausea, drowsiness and impaired judgment. In a light airplane, an exhaust/cabin-heat leak is a classic concern; ventilate and land as soon as practical if CO exposure is suspected.

NTSB Accident / Incident Quick Reference

The NTSB is separate from the FAA and has its own reporting definitions and requirements.

Accident vs. Incident — NTSB Part 830

Aircraft Accident

An occurrence associated with aircraft operation in which a person suffers death or serious injury, or the aircraft receives substantial damage, within the definition provided by NTSB rules.

Aircraft Incident

An occurrence other than an accident associated with aircraft operation that affects or could affect operational safety.

Serious Injury — Examples in the Guides

  • Hospitalization for more than 48 hours beginning within 7 days of the injury.
  • Specified fractures other than simple fractures of fingers, toes or nose.
  • Severe hemorrhage or nerve/muscle/tendon damage.
  • Internal-organ injury.
  • Specified second- or third-degree burns or burns covering more than 5% of the body.

Substantial Damage

Damage/failure adversely affecting structural strength, performance or flight characteristics and normally requiring major repair/replacement of the affected component, subject to the full NTSB definition and exclusions.

Rapid Review Questions

Use these as an oral-review prompt. Click each question to reveal the answer.

What are the three different “night” time references?

Logging: end of evening civil twilight to beginning of morning civil twilight. Passenger landing currency: 1 hour after sunset to 1 hour before sunrise. Required position/anti-collision lights: sunset to sunrise.

What does “cleared into the Bravo” mean?

It is the explicit ATC clearance required before VFR entry into Class B airspace; two-way communication alone is not sufficient.

What is the difference between a course, heading and track?

Course is the intended ground path; heading is where the nose points; track is the actual ground path.

Why does stall speed increase in a level turn?

More total lift is required to keep the vertical lift component equal to weight, increasing load factor; the higher load factor increases stall speed.

What happens with a blocked static port?

The altimeter freezes, VSI reads zero, and the airspeed indicator becomes erroneous.

What are the four left-turning tendencies?

Torque, gyroscopic precession, spiraling slipstream and P-factor.

What does NWKRAFT stand for?

NOTAMs, Weather, Known traffic delays, Runway lengths, Alternatives, Fuel requirements, Takeoff/landing performance.

What is the VFR-day fuel reserve?

Enough to reach the first intended landing point, then fly for at least 30 minutes at normal cruise. At night, 45 minutes.

What are the four transponder codes every Private Pilot should recognize?

1200 VFR, 7500 unlawful interference, 7600 lost communications, 7700 emergency.

What does a VOR radial represent?

A magnetic bearing FROM the VOR station.
